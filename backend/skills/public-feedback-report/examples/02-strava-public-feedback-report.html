TL;DR

Everything below is something a product, design or engineering team can actually change. Complaints about price, billing and support were also collected, but they are kept separate further down so they don't drown out the build list.

Three problems are doing most of the damage right now — plus the one people are actually leaving over, and the one that only just appeared:

#1"I did that ride. Strava says I didn't — and I can't get it undone." January's leaderboard cleanup was a genuine win, but the system that spots fake activities also catches real ones. A GPS glitch is enough to trigger it, and the only way to fix it is to raise a support ticket. It hits the people who care most about the leaderboard hardest. "numerous legitimate activities flagged" — Trustpilot, 24 Jan 2026
#2"I get locked out, and I'm not sure my ride even saved." People are locked out of their accounts with no way to get back in on their own, Android keeps throwing server errors, and on 10 June login and syncing went down at the same time. Five people described being completely stuck — the worst outcome in the whole set. "locked out of my account due to a problem" — Trustpilot, 20 May 2026
#3"The apps I connect to Strava can't show my data anymore — not even to my coach." After we changed the rules for other apps, people who used Strava as the link between their watch and their training tools lost that. For them, being that link was the thing they were paying for. "killing half the reason I'm on Strava" — mtbr forum, Nov 2024
#4What people are actually leaving over: not being able to share their data. Two people said plainly that they are leaving, and both gave the same reason — the change to how other apps can use their data. Both named Garmin as where they are going. Everything else in this report is people complaining while staying. This is the only thing anyone said out loud they'd quit over.
#5What's brand new since January: three things that weren't here before. Real activities being wrongly flagged (started right after the January cleanup) · segments not carrying over when a ride is logged as an e-bike, so people lose their personal bests · notification settings being switched off but emails still arriving. None of these existed in last year's feedback.

What we'd do first: let people appeal a flagged activity themselves, in the app. It stops January's win turning into a grievance, it takes work off a support queue people already struggle to reach, and it protects the exact athletes who keep the segment system alive.

Recommendations

Everything here can be built by the product, design or engineering team. Ordered by how many people it affects, how badly it hurts them, and how much it matters to what the business is trying to protect this year.

1Self-serve appeal for flagged activitiesHigh impact
"My real rides get flagged and I can't get the flag removed."
Why: the January backfill retired our longest-running credibility complaint — a genuine asset. False positives now land on exactly the athletes who care most, and the only remedy is a support ticket into a queue with a 1.5★ public reputation. An in-product appeal with a visible decision, plus published false-positive handling, protects the gain and removes the dependency on a channel we don't control.
Moves: power-user retention ↑ · leaderboard trust ↑ · support volume ↓
2A consented third-party data path — starting with coach accessHigh impact
"The apps I connected can't show my data anymore — not even to my coach."
Why: this is the only thing anyone said outright they were leaving over, and Garmin is the named beneficiary. Letting people choose to share their own data — starting with their coach, which is the clearest thing they asked for — wins most of the goodwill back without undoing what the rule was there to protect. Check this first: the complaints are from late 2024, so confirm what the product actually does today before scoping anything.
Moves: power-user retention ↑ · ecosystem partners ↑ · data-serious segment share
3Lockout self-recovery and a no-lost-activity guaranteeHigh impact
"I get locked out, and I'm not sure my activity made it in."
Why: five people described being completely stuck — the worst outcome anyone reported — and the 10 June outage put login and sync down together. Two fixes: account recovery that doesn't route through a ticket, and a visible local-buffer guarantee so a ride recorded during an outage is provably safe.
Moves: blocking incidents ↓ · Android store rating ↑ · trust in recording ↑
4Take safety-relevant map layers out from behind the tierCheap trust
"The map that keeps me safe running at night costs extra."
Why: this is a packaging decision, and packaging is product's to change. Gating a safety surface is the single most quotable criticism in the current press cycle, and it's cheap to reverse. Pair it with making it obvious in the app what each plan actually includes — right now people can't tell.
Moves: install→activation ↑ · press narrative ↑ · perceived fairness ↑
5A real off switch for AI insightsCheap win
"Strava writes a paragraph about my run and I can't turn it off."
Why: not many people, but they've been asking since 2024, and they're the committed subscribers who post in our own forum. Worth noting the other side: one person this year asked us to turn AI insights back on after they were switched off. People don't want it gone — they want to choose.
Moves: subscriber sentiment ↑ · forum goodwill ↑

Also worth a look — not for the product team

13 of the 52 posts we found — a quarter of everything — aren't things the product team can fix. We kept them out of the section above so they wouldn't crowd out the build list, but they're real, they're clustered, and not one of them was positive. Here are the three biggest, and who owns them:

Support
Users can't reach a human, and tickets close without resolution
The biggest cluster of the three: multi-week replies, generic one-line answers, and an AI triage layer that resists escalation. It's the reason our Trustpilot rating is 1.5★ and our BBB rating is D- — the first thing someone sees when they search our name before subscribing. It also makes every product problem above look worse than it is, because from the user's side a bug they can't report and a bug we won't fix feel identical.

Renewal and discount disputes where the account page displays one rate and the card is charged another, and support restates the timeline rather than addressing the contradiction. Distinct from "too expensive" — this is an operations failure, not a pricing opinion.

Next steps

#1 and #3 are the pair to move on. Both are about a user losing something they earned — a segment result, or an activity — with no way to get it back themselves. Fixing that is also the fastest reduction in load on the support queue described above.

Where this came from and how much to trust it.

How this was made. Prepared 21 July 2026. We logged each piece of feedback separately — 52 in total — and tagged each one with whether the product team can fix it and who owns it. Nothing was merged or combined: if three people said the same thing, that's three entries, because how often something comes up matters. Every quote is real, dated and linked to where it was posted. Nothing here is made up. The full list is attached as strava-feedback-records.json.

The big limitation. We found this feedback by searching the web, not by pulling it directly from each platform. That matters: most of the Reddit and app-store comments reached us via news articles, so we're over-weighted towards whatever a journalist found quotable. Trustpilot we read directly, and Trustpilot is where angry people go. So: trust the list of problems, treat the ranking as a rough order, and be careful comparing one platform to another.

What the percentages actually mean. Every percentage is out of the 52 posts we found — never out of Strava's users. If it says 64% negative, that means 25 of the 39 product posts were negative. It does not mean 64% of our users are unhappy, and it shouldn't be quoted that way. We have no way of knowing how many people had a problem and never posted about it.

About the quotes. We quote one short line per source and summarise the rest in our own words. That's a limit on how much of someone else's writing we reproduce, not a gap in what we read — everything was read and logged.

How old the feedback is. 17 of the 39 product issues are from 2024 or 2025, mostly about the data-sharing rules and the AI write-ups. Some may already be fixed. We kept them because people still quote them as current, but any recommendation based on them says to check the facts first.

Where sources disagree. Google Play's rating is given as 4.4 by one source and 4.6 by another for the same period, so we show both rather than picking one. The 4.8★ App Store figure comes from a 2025 source and may be out of date. The positive / neutral / negative labels were assigned by hand, post by post — no sentiment model was used.

Checks for fake feedback. We saw no sign of bots or organised review attacks in the negative feedback. We did find "best Strava alternatives" articles written by rival apps as marketing — we kept them but marked them, and they don't count towards anyone leaving. We only counted someone as leaving if they said so outright: two people. Being annoyed was never treated as leaving.

How often to run this. Best run monthly, so each edition can be compared against the one before. This is the first, so there's nothing to compare it to yet — which is why the "what's changed" sections read as history rather than as a month-on-month move.
